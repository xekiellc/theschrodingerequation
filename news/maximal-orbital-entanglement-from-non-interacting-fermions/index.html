Slater Determinants Can Achieve Maximal Orbital Entanglement

A preprint proves non-interacting fermion states can saturate entanglement bounds, reframing mean-field states as quantum information resources.

October 23, 2026

A preprint posted to arXiv — not yet peer-reviewed — argues that Slater determinants, the mean-field wave functions routinely used as starting points in quantum chemistry and condensed matter physics, are not the entanglement-poor objects they are often assumed to be. The authors prove that such states can carry up to min{d_A, d_B, N, d−N} ebits of orbital entanglement across a bipartition of a lattice, and that this bound matches the maximum achievable by any fermionic state when enough particles and holes are present.

The result turns on a careful distinction between particle entanglement and orbital entanglement. Slater determinants are, by construction, free of particle-particle correlations, but the paper shows this imposes no corresponding ceiling on how entangled the occupied orbitals can be across a spatial bipartition. For arbitrary free (non-interacting) fermionic states, the authors also derive the maximal orbital total correlation in closed form for all bipartitions, for the finest partition, and for all partitions of up to five sites, making use of new majorization relations for Hermitian matrices.

If the claims survive peer review, the practical implication is notable: the same mean-field states that quantum chemists use to minimize entanglement complexity — by rotating orbitals to compress correlations into a small active space — can, under a different orbital rotation, be tuned to maximize entanglement as a computational resource. The authors suggest this positions free fermionic states as viable inputs for quantum information protocols, without requiring the overhead of genuinely correlated many-body preparation.
