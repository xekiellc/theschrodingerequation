New optical system pushes spatial light modulation to 84 megaframes per second for cold atoms

A preprint reports an SLM architecture combining high resolution, low disorder, and unprecedented frame rates for programmable ultracold-atom arrays.

August 18, 2026

Programmable arrays of ultracold atoms depend entirely on precisely shaped laser fields for local control — every gate, every readout, every rearrangement of atoms is ultimately an optics problem. A new preprint posted to arXiv (not yet peer-reviewed) describes a spatial light modulation system designed to advance several of those optical axes simultaneously: the authors report arbitrary pattern generation with 10⁻³ intensity resolution, a spatial resolution of 83 × 52 beam waists, and a frame rate exceeding 84 megaframes per second — a figure that places optical switching well below the timescales of relevant atomic dynamics.

The architecture incorporates a 40 GHz electro-optic modulator that provides access to an 11 × 52 waist sub-region at particularly high speed. The authors argue this combination of speed, resolution, and low disorder is complementary to existing approaches in scale while improving the other figures of merit. To demonstrate practical utility, they develop and numerically validate a scheme for fully programmable Hubbard models with time-dependent control over local chemical potentials, tunneling amplitudes, on-site interactions, and artificial magnetic flux patterns — parameters relevant to open questions in strongly correlated condensed matter physics.

The system is also claimed to support fast, arbitrary 2D tweezer permutations, which the authors suggest could decouple optical hardware constraints from the design of high-rate quantum error-correcting codes. As an unreviewed preprint, independent validation of the reported frame rates and the proposed Hubbard-model control scheme remains outstanding.
