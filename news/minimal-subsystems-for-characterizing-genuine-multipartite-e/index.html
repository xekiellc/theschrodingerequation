Single-qubit measurements may suffice to quantify genuine multipartite entanglement

A preprint argues that for typical N-qubit states, the genuine multipartite concurrence requires only N single-site purity measurements.

October 12, 2026

Quantifying genuine multipartite entanglement (GME) — the kind that cannot be reduced to entanglement among subgroups — has long appeared computationally daunting. Measures defined through optimizations over all possible bipartitions nominally demand joint measurements across large subsystems and an exponentially growing number of measurement settings. A new preprint on arXiv (not yet peer-reviewed) introduces two operational quantities, the GME evaluation length and GME evaluation number, to make this cost precise: they capture, respectively, the largest subsystem that must be jointly measured and how many distinct subsystems need to be probed.

Taking the genuine multipartite concurrence (GMC) as their test case, the authors claim that for N-qubit Haar-random pure states the GMC can be fully evaluated from just N single-qubit purity measurements — one per site. Rather than scanning an exponentially large set of bipartitions, only the N single-site bipartitions turn out to be necessary for typical states. The authors report numerical support for this behavior in states produced by local random quantum circuits, suggesting the result is not confined to the analytically tractable Haar-random ensemble.

If borne out by peer review, the findings reframe the practical overhead of GME quantification: the formal worst-case complexity suggested by a measure's definition need not reflect the cost for physically relevant states. The authors position measurement complexity as a complementary operational lens through which multipartite entanglement structure can be studied, though the scope of states for which the reduction holds — and the precise experimental requirements for measuring single-site purities — will require further scrutiny.
