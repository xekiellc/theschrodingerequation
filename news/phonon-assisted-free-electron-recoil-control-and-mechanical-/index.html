Optomechanical membrane proposed as phonon-mediated control layer for free electrons

A theoretical preprint argues vibrating membranes can steer electron recoil and sense localized forces via phonon-assisted light scattering.

October 21, 2026

A preprint posted to arXiv — not yet peer-reviewed — proposes that an optomechanical membrane vibrating inside a cavity could serve as a programmable interface between free electrons and light. The theoretical framework describes how transverse membrane motion scatters cavity photons into high-momentum evanescent sidebands. Those sidebands can, in principle, phase-match with electrons traveling in vacuum nearby, transferring momentum and redistributing the electrons across discrete recoil orders.

The authors argue that because the interaction strength depends on the vibration amplitude, an external force that perturbs the membrane is directly encoded in the resulting electron recoil spectrum. Under their modeled conditions, reading that spectrum out electronically could yield a higher signal-to-noise ratio for detecting localized stochastic forces than a Gaussian optical homodyne measurement operating at the same detected probe rate — a claim that, if borne out experimentally, would have implications for precision force sensing at sub-diffraction scales.

The work is presented as a theoretical basis for two potential application directions: mechanically reconfigurable free-electron optics, where vibration amplitude tunes electron-beam shaping in vacuum, and mode-selective mechanical sensing, where spatial specificity of the electron probe isolates individual mechanical modes. No experimental results are reported; the findings rest entirely on the theoretical model described, and independent experimental and peer review have not yet occurred.
