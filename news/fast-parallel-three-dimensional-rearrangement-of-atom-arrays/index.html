GPU-computed holograms enable parallel 3D atom-array assembly in under 20 ms

An analytic hologram superposition method assembles 100-atom arrays with 99.31% per-atom survival, computed faster than SLM refresh rates.

October 15, 2026

Assembling large, defect-free arrays of neutral atoms — a prerequisite for scalable quantum computing and simulation — requires rapidly correcting the random gaps left by stochastic trap loading. A preprint posted to arXiv (not yet peer-reviewed) describes a parallel rearrangement scheme in which all atoms are moved simultaneously by a spatial light modulator (SLM) displaying analytically computed holograms, rather than shuttling atoms one at a time as conventional approaches do.

The method constructs each hologram by superimposing the complex optical fields for every individual trap, then applies a linear phase ramp to each trap as it travels to its target — a formulation that treats trap positions as continuous variables rather than grid points, preserving sub-pixel positioning accuracy. Running on a single GPU, each intermediate hologram is computed in 0.284 ms, well within the SLM's 0.696 ms refresh cycle; the authors use the remaining processing margin to iteratively equalize trap intensities, achieving non-uniformity of 1.24% at no additional time cost. The paper also demonstrates axial (out-of-plane) atom transport, illustrated by a protocol in which one layer of atoms leapfrogs over another.

Starting from 256 stochastically loaded sites of ⁸⁷Rb, the system assembles a 100-atom array in under 20 ms with a per-atom rearrangement survival probability of 99.31(12)% and a measured temperature increase of only 9(3) μK against a trap depth of 480 μK. The authors argue the parallel architecture scales more favorably than sequential methods as array sizes grow, though independent validation awaits peer review.
