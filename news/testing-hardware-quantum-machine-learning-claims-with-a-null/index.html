Null-circuit test exposes job-fingerprinting artifact in quantum ML fill prediction

A preregistered protocol finds that IBM Heron hardware signatures, not quantum features, explain a reported trading-prediction gain.

October 30, 2026

This is an unreviewed preprint posted to arXiv and has not yet undergone peer review. The authors propose and apply a five-step validation protocol for quantum machine learning pipelines that use hardware expectation values as features for classical models on tabular data. Their target is a previously reported HSBC–IBM study claiming that noisy quantum hardware produced gains in predicting bond-trade fill probabilities that ideal simulation could not replicate.

The central mechanism the authors identify is job-level fingerprinting: because a quantum processor's noise profile drifts between calibration cycles, each submitted job carries a statistically distinguishable hardware signature. If records sharing an outcome are batched into the same jobs and those jobs appear on both sides of a train/test split, a data-free null circuit—one encoding no information whatsoever—can predict outcomes well above chance purely by matching fingerprints. The authors demonstrate this on a 129-qubit chain of an IBM Heron processor. When job assignment is controlled so that no job straddles the train/test boundary, the quantum features yield no advantage over classical baselines, and the device's output fidelity is consistent with shot noise alone.

The proposed protocol requires less than one hour of processor time and is intended to generalize to any hardware-feature or quantum-kernel pipeline applied to tabular data. The authors pre-registered their analysis before running the experiment, which strengthens the inferential standing of the null result, though independent replication and formal peer review remain necessary before the findings can be considered settled.
