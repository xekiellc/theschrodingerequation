Geodesic algorithm tightens entanglement and capacity bounds for Gaussian light

A new framework computes Gaussian-restricted entanglement measures with polynomial bit-complexity, bounding quantum and secret-key rates under loss.

October 13, 2026

An unreviewed preprint posted to arXiv proposes a unified framework for calculating two entanglement measures—the relative entropy of entanglement and the Rains entanglement—when restricted to Gaussian states, the class of quantum optical states fully characterized by their first and second moments. Loss and noise make distributing entanglement through optical channels difficult, and tight upper bounds on what any protocol can extract are correspondingly hard to compute. The authors address this by revealing a convex geometric structure underlying these measures, centered on matrix geometric means and geodesics in the space of positive-definite matrices.

The framework yields several technical advances the paper claims: unique optimal reference states, an additivity result for independent Gaussian inputs, and an iterative algorithm with an explicit geometric convergence rate. Crucially, the authors argue the bit-complexity of their procedure scales polynomially in the desired accuracy for any fixed rational input covariance matrix—a stronger computational guarantee than previously available. They also show how teleportation simulation and a convex optimization over passive-loss channel cores translate these entanglement bounds into limits on quantum communication capacity and secret-key rates under energy constraints, applicable even to arbitrary two-way classically assisted protocols.

Two- and three-mode worked examples compare the new bounds against known achievable rates, with the authors reporting agreement at pure-loss benchmarks. The paper additionally addresses the gap between Gaussian-restricted and fully unrestricted entanglement measures, offering a photon-number truncation method with controlled error for the latter. As with all arXiv preprints, these results have not yet undergone peer review, and independent verification of the complexity claims and numerical methods will be needed before the bounds can be considered firmly established.
