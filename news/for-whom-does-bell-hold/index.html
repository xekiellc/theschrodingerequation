Quantum vs. classical evolution: a Bell-type test without Bell observables

A preprint proposes statistical correlation signatures that distinguish quantum Hamiltonian evolution from classical mimics, even when standard Bell tests are inaccessible.

September 9, 2026

Standard Bell inequality tests require the ability to freely choose and measure incompatible observables — a luxury not always available. In cosmology, for instance, primordial density fluctuations are thought to originate from quantum vacuum fluctuations, yet every accessible observable is effectively classical, making a conventional Bell test impossible. A new preprint posted to arXiv (not yet peer-reviewed) argues that the pattern of statistical correlations among available observables can nevertheless serve as a surrogate Bell-type witness for the quantum nature of time evolution.

The authors analyze closed systems undergoing Hamiltonian evolution and identify a structural difference: quantum vacuum correlations are generically time-independent and governed by the energy gap between the ground state and excited states, whereas classical Hamiltonian systems exhibit apparent poles at physical frequencies. Near those poles, classical evolution becomes resonance-dominated, producing time-dependent correlations that ultimately decay via dephasing — a signature absent in the quantum case. The paper claims these features constitute a diagnostic that is not reproducible by classical Hamiltonian dynamics.

The authors further argue that this correlation-based criterion is distinct from other known markers of quantum behavior, and they illustrate its scope through applications to quantum optics, quantum walks, and quantum search algorithms. The results, if they survive peer review, could sharpen the conceptual toolkit for assessing quantumness in systems — from early-universe cosmology to quantum information — where direct Bell tests remain out of reach. Readers should note that all claims are those of the preprint authors and have not yet undergone formal peer review.
